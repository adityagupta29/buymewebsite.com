Why Every Small Restaurant in India Needs Its Own Website, Not Just Zomato & Swiggy

Zomato and Swiggy built the habit of ordering food online in India, but every order through them costs restaurant owners 20 to 30 percent in commission. Here is how a simple website of your own can protect your margins and your customer relationships.

September 2026 7 min read Team BuyMeWebsite

Ask any restaurant owner in India how business has changed in the last five years, and Zomato and Swiggy will come up within the first minute. These platforms brought millions of hungry customers online and made food ordering effortless. But that convenience comes at a steep price: most restaurants pay 20 to 30 percent commission on every single order, before accounting for packaging costs, discounts the platforms push you to run, and the ranking games you have to play just to stay visible.

For a restaurant running on thin margins, giving away a quarter of every order is not a small cost, it's often the difference between a profitable month and a break-even one. And the deeper problem is this: when a customer orders through Zomato or Swiggy, the platform owns that customer, not you. You have no way to message them directly, remind them about a new dish, or reward them for ordering again. Their loyalty belongs to the app's discounts, not your kitchen.

The Real Cost of Depending Only on Delivery Apps

Beyond commission, there's a longer-term risk. Delivery platforms regularly change their algorithms, introduce new fees, and push restaurants to run discounts just to stay visible in search results. A restaurant that depends entirely on these apps has effectively handed over control of its own customer relationships and pricing to a company whose incentives don't always match its own.

This doesn't mean you should leave Zomato or Swiggy, they're still useful for discovery. But treating them as your only channel means every single customer relationship runs through a middleman who takes a cut and can change the rules anytime.

Even if only a third of your regular customers start ordering directly through your website instead of the apps, that's a meaningful chunk of revenue that stops leaking out in commission every month, and WhatsApp still has a role, as an order alert so you never miss one, not as the checkout itself.

Building Repeat Customers Instead of One-Time App Orders

The biggest advantage of your own website is what happens after the first order. Once a customer has ordered directly from you once, you have their number. You can send a WhatsApp message when you launch a new dish, offer a loyalty discount for regulars, or simply remind them you exist on a slow Tuesday evening. None of this is possible when every order flows through an app that keeps that customer data to itself.

Restaurants that combine delivery apps for new customer discovery with their own ordering website for repeat customers consistently end up with healthier margins than those relying on apps alone.

Common Mistakes Restaurants Make Going Digital

Many restaurant owners who do attempt to go digital make a few avoidable mistakes. The most common is letting menu prices drift out of sync between the delivery app, the physical menu, and word of mouth, which confuses customers and erodes trust. Another is ignoring basic local search altogether: when someone searches "biryani near me" or "best cafe in [area]", restaurants with no website and an incomplete Google Business listing simply don't show up, no matter how good the food actually is.

A third mistake is collecting zero customer information from dine-in guests. Every table that scans a QR code menu, or every walk-in customer, is a missed opportunity to capture a phone number for future offers, unless that QR code actually leads somewhere useful, like your website, where you can prompt a WhatsApp opt-in.

How a Website and Delivery Apps Work Together, Not Against Each Other

The smartest approach isn't choosing between delivery apps and your own website, it's using each for what it does best. Keep using Zomato and Swiggy for what they're genuinely good at: getting discovered by people who've never heard of you. But once a customer has ordered from you once, whether through an app or in person, your job is to move that relationship onto a channel you control.

A simple QR code on your table tents and takeaway bags, linking straight to your website's menu and checkout, does exactly this. Over a few months, this quietly builds a direct customer base that orders from you without the delivery app's commission or interference, while you still benefit from app-driven discovery for new customers.

Turning First-Time Online Orders Into Regulars

Getting a customer to order from your website once is only half the win, the real value shows up when they come back a second and third time without needing an app's discount to nudge them. A short WhatsApp broadcast list built from your direct orders lets you announce a new weekend special, a festive menu, or simply a "we miss you" message to someone who hasn't ordered in a few weeks, something no delivery app will ever let you do with their customers.

Small touches compound here: a thank-you message after every direct order, a modest discount on someone's second website order, or simply remembering a regular's usual order. None of this is possible when a customer only exists as an anonymous order ID inside a delivery app's system.
